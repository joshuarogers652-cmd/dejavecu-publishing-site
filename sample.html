Chapter One
It’s Happening Again

Bennett Research Notes — Undated

“Memory rarely announces its failures. Sometimes it simply arrives too soon.”

• • •

I woke before the alarm. I often did, though not consistently enough to call it a habit. Just often enough to make the moment feel personal, as though my body had received a message my mind wasn’t allowed to read.

The room was still dark, but not completely. Queens had a way of refusing true darkness. Pale orange streetlight slipped between the blinds and striped the wall above my desk, cutting through the shadows in long, thin bars. My textbooks sat stacked beside my laptop: Criminal Behavior, Abnormal Psychology, and Introduction to Legal Studies. A yellow legal pad rested on top, its first page covered in my small, careful handwriting.

Patterns are only strange until they repeat.

I stared at the ceiling and counted down.

Three. Two. One.

My phone alarm screamed.

6:14 a.m.

I shut it off without looking and remained still for a few seconds, listening to the familiar sounds of the building coming to life. The radiator ticked beside the window. A pipe knocked somewhere behind the wall. Outside, a truck backed up, its warning signal cutting through the morning quiet. A dog barked twice.

Then a man on the sidewalk below laughed too loudly into his phone.

Right on time.

My throat tightened. It’s happening again.

I hated that phrase. Hated how dramatic it sounded and how easily it arrived, before thought, before logic, before I could remind myself that déjà vu was normal. Everyone experienced it. The brain misfired. Memory mislabeled the present as the past. That was all.

That was what Professor Bennett would say. It was what I would say if this were happening to someone else.

I sat up slowly, almost afraid that moving too quickly might somehow lock the day into place.

My room was small but organized with nearly aggressive precision. The bed stood against one wall, my desk beside the window, and a laundry basket occupied the corner. Above the dresser hung a framed Michael Jackson poster: black fedora, white socks, impossible lean. My mother had bought it for me when I was twelve, after months of hearing “Human Nature” through the bedroom wall.

“You know he died before you were old enough to know who he was, right?” Maya had asked the first time she saw it.

I’d shrugged. “Some things are before time.”

Maya had laughed. I’d meant it.

I swung my legs toward the floor, preparing to stand, but stopped just as my right foot was about to touch the cold wood.

No. Left.

I lowered my left foot instead. It was a small change, meaningless probably, and stupid if I was being fair about it. Yet my breathing eased the moment I made it. Tiny rebellions helped.

I crossed the cold floor to the kitchenette and reached for the chipped white mug near the sink. Halfway there, my fingers stopped. A thin crack ran along the ceramic near the handle. I stared at it, unsettled by a familiarity I couldn’t explain.

I knew that crack.

Not remembered. Knew.

I withdrew my hand and chose the blue mug instead.

Change the cup. Change the day.

My subconscious offered suggestions like that sometimes. They weren’t voices, exactly, but thoughts with an unfamiliar weight, as though they had arrived from somewhere outside my own reasoning.

The coffee machine coughed awake. Wearing an oversized St. John’s sweatshirt and black sleep shorts, I stood watching the dark liquid drip into my mug. Outside, morning gathered itself over the city. A distant siren rose above the traffic before fading away.

My phone buzzed on the counter.

MAYA: You up?

EMMA: Unfortunately.

MAYA: Don’t be weird today.

I stared at the message until another appeared.

MAYA: Actually be medium weird. I have court club later and need entertainment.

I smiled despite myself.

Maya Patel lived two floors below, although she treated my apartment like an extension of her own. She was pre-law, loud, brilliant, and chronically late, the kind of person who entered every room as though destiny had personally invited her. I envied that confidence.

I lifted my coffee and took a sip before it had cooled.

Too hot.

I’d known it would be, but the coffee burned my tongue anyway.

“Idiot,” I whispered.

Again.

The word sent a familiar chill up my spine. I set the mug down, then deliberately moved it three inches to the left.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened.

I showered, dressed, brushed my hair, and tied it back loosely. I chose dark jeans, white sneakers, a fitted black jacket, and a gray scarf. The forecast called for rain, although the sky outside looked clear.

At the apartment door, I checked that I had everything: keys, phone, wallet, student ID. I checked the lock twice, then a third time.

Not because I was anxious.

Because yesterday—

No. Not yesterday.

Because once, somewhere, some version of this morning had ended with me standing outside without my keys.

I closed my eyes. “That didn’t happen,” I told myself.

The apartment gave no opinion.

I stepped into the hallway just as Mrs. Alvarez from 3B opened her door, carrying a small trash bag and wearing her usual pink slippers.

“Morning, sweetheart.”

I smiled automatically. “Morning. Elevator’s slow today.”

Mrs. Alvarez blinked. Behind us, the elevator groaned and stopped between floors. A red light began blinking above its doors.

She looked at the elevator, then back at me. “How’d you know that?”

My fingers twitched at my side. Without thinking, I scratched the inside of my wrist, one quick movement that was over almost before I noticed it.

“Old building,” I said.

Mrs. Alvarez accepted the explanation. People generally accepted simple explanations when they were offered quickly enough.

I took the stairs.

Outside, Queens was waking in layers. Bodega gates rattled open along the sidewalk, and steam rose from a manhole near the corner. A delivery cyclist darted between cars with suicidal confidence. Somewhere above the street, someone played salsa through an open second-floor window. The morning air carried the mixed scents of wet concrete, roasted coffee, and exhaust.

I loved New York at this hour, before the day became too crowded with other people’s intentions. I also distrusted it. Too many variables.

At the corner, I stopped at the crosswalk with six other pedestrians. Opposite us, a cyclist in a green jacket waited with one foot balanced on the curb.

I glanced at him.

He drops the phone.

The thought arrived whole, without warning or explanation. The cyclist reached into his pocket, and I looked away.

No. Don’t.

The light changed, and the crowd began crossing. Behind me, something clattered against the pavement.

“Damn it,” the cyclist muttered.

I kept walking, although my pulse had begun pounding in my ears. Coincidence. Pattern recognition. Micro-movement.

I’d probably noticed the phone slipping from his pocket before he reached for it. My brain had processed the information faster than my conscious mind. That was entirely possible. People made predictions like that every day without realizing it.

That was all. That was always all.

Until it wasn’t.

By the time I reached the subway platform, the station was crowded enough to feel like a lung. Commuters breathed and shifted around one another, checking their phones and avoiding eye contact while they waited for the train. I stood near a pillar with peeling paint and watched the tracks.

A woman in a tan coat stood near the platform’s edge. Too close. My shoulders tightened.

Don’t.

The lights of an approaching train appeared in the tunnel. The woman swayed slightly, and I stepped forward without thinking, bumping her shoulder.

She stumbled backward. “Excuse me?” she snapped.

Heat rushed into my face. “I’m sorry. I—”

The train thundered into the station, sending a blast of wind across the platform. Hair, loose paper, and dust whipped through the air. The woman glared at me, but I stared at the yellow warning strip.

Nothing happened.

No fall. No scream. No tragedy.

Just a woman who was understandably angry about being shoved by a stranger.

I swallowed. “I’m sorry,” I said again.

She muttered something under her breath and walked away.

I boarded the train and stood near the doors, gripping a metal pole with one hand and the strap of my backpack with the other.

A false alarm. Maybe that was worse.

If the feeling could be wrong, then perhaps none of it meant anything. Perhaps every prediction, every familiar sound, every moment of certainty was nothing more than my mind searching for order where none existed.

Or perhaps it meant something worse. I didn’t know which possibility frightened me more.

By the time I reached St. John’s, the campus looked almost peaceful beneath the gray morning sky. Brick buildings stood among bare winter branches, while students hurried between classes carrying coffee, backpacks, headphones, and the quiet confidence of people who knew where they were going.

The university had a rhythm I’d learned quickly: bells ringing, footsteps crossing the courtyard, elevator doors opening and closing, professors cutting across lawns even where signs specifically told them not to.

Maya was waiting outside St. Augustine Hall with two coffees and an expression of exaggerated betrayal.

“You’re late.”

I checked my phone. “I’m four minutes early.”

“Emotionally late.” Maya handed me a coffee. “You have that face.”

“What face?”

“The I-have-seen-the-future-and-it’s-annoying face.”

I accepted the cup but didn’t drink.

Maya narrowed her eyes. “What happened?”

“Nothing.”

“Nothing has a lot of symptoms with you.”

I almost told her about the woman on the platform, the cyclist’s phone, and the elevator stopping between floors. I wanted to explain how the morning had seemed to unfold around me like a page I’d already read, each moment arriving exactly where it belonged. But I could already imagine how the explanation would sound.

Instead, I shrugged. “I didn’t sleep great.”

Maya’s expression softened, although only slightly. “You never sleep great.”

“That’s not true.”

“Emma, your under-eye circles have under-eye circles.”

I smiled, and we headed into the building together.

Forensic Psychology met in a lecture hall that smelled faintly of old carpet and dry-erase markers. I chose my usual seat, three rows from the back on the aisle. Maya dropped into the chair beside me and immediately began scrolling through notes for another class.

Professor Evelyn Bennett entered at exactly 8:02. She had short silver hair, wore a black blazer, and carried herself with the no-nonsense authority of someone who could silence a room simply by removing her glasses.

Without introducing the day’s topic, she turned toward the board and wrote a single sentence.

MEMORY IS NOT A RECORDING.

I stared at the words. My chest tightened.

Professor Bennett turned to face the class. “Most people believe memory works like a camera. It does not. Memory is reconstruction. Every time you remember something, you rebuild it. And every time you rebuild it, you risk changing it.”

My pen hovered above my notebook. The words felt aimed directly at me.

Professor Bennett continued. “This is why eyewitness testimony, despite its emotional power, is often dangerously unreliable.”

I lowered my pen and wrote:

Memory is reconstruction.

Then, beneath it:

What if the reconstruction is accurate?

I scratched the inside of my wrist. Maya noticed but said nothing.

Professor Bennett clicked to the next slide, revealing a photograph of a crowded street frozen in motion.

“Let’s test perception. Look carefully. In a moment, I’ll ask what you remember.”

I studied the image. A man in a red hoodie stood near a woman pushing a stroller. A taxi occupied part of the street. There was a dog, an umbrella, and a boy near the curb holding a blue balloon.

My gaze settled on the boy. His fingers were wrapped around the string.

The balloon string was about to slip.

My stomach dropped. Professor Bennett hadn’t asked the question yet.

“The boy loses the balloon,” I whispered.

The room went quiet. Maya turned toward me, and Professor Bennett stopped speaking.

On the screen, nothing moved. It was only a photograph. The boy remained frozen near the curb, his small fist still wrapped around the string.

Professor Bennett’s expression sharpened. “I hadn’t asked the question yet, Miss Rogers.”

My mouth went dry. Every student in the room seemed to be looking at me. I stared at the boy on the screen, unable to explain why I’d been so certain of something that hadn’t happened.

“I’m sorry,” I said. My voice sounded distant, almost unfamiliar. “I thought you did.”

Professor Bennett held my gaze for another moment. Then she offered a polite smile and turned back to the class.

“No harm done. But that is an interesting example of anticipatory bias.”

A few students laughed. Maya did not.

I lowered my eyes to my notebook, wishing I could disappear into the familiar lines of my own handwriting.

Then I stopped.

On the page, beneath my notes, was a drawing I didn’t remember making.

A balloon.

Blue.

Floating upward.

I stared at it, my fingers tightening around the pen.

Beneath the drawing, written in my own handwriting, were three words.

It’s happening again.

End of Chapter One
